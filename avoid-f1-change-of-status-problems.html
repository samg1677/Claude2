An F1 visa change of status lets you switch to student status without leaving the United States. You file Form I-539 with USCIS, and you stay in the country while it is decided. It sounds simple, but most of the problems students run into come from timing and small filing mistakes, not from the rules themselves.

This guide walks through the process step by step and points out where professional help makes a difference.

Quick answer

The most common F1 change of status problems are filing after your current status has expired and starting classes before USCIS approves the change. Long processing times that push you past your I-20 program start date are close behind. Visitors in B-1/B-2 status face extra scrutiny, and incomplete financial evidence is a frequent cause of requests for evidence.

You can avoid most of these by filing early, keeping your current status valid, choosing a school with frequent start dates and getting legal advice from a licensed immigration attorney before you file.

How F1 visa change of status works, step by step

Each step below includes the mistake that most often trips students up at that point.

1. Confirm you are eligible to change status in the U.S.

You must have been lawfully admitted, your current status must still be valid and you must not have violated its terms, for example by working without authorization.

Watch out: people admitted under the Visa Waiver Program (ESTA) cannot change status in the U.S. Some J-1 holders subject to the two-year home residency requirement cannot change to F-1 either.

2. Get admitted to an SEVP-certified school and receive an initial I-20

The school's Designated School Official (DSO) issues the I-20 with "change of status" as the reason.

Watch out: your financial documents must cover the amount on the I-20. Weak or outdated bank statements are one of the most common reasons USCIS asks for more evidence.

3. Pay the I-901 SEVIS fee

Keep the payment receipt. You will include it with your application.

Watch out: the SEVIS fee is tied to your I-20's SEVIS ID. If the school issues a new I-20 with a new ID, check with the DSO before paying again.

4. File Form I-539 with USCIS before your current status expires

Include your I-20, SEVIS fee receipt, I-94, passport, financial evidence and a copy of your current visa. Check the USCIS I-539 page for the current fee and form edition.

Watch out: if your status expires before USCIS receives your application, USCIS generally cannot approve it. This is the one mistake that is very hard to fix.

5. Consider premium processing if your start date is close

Change of status requests to F-1 are eligible for premium processing through Form I-907. The fee rose to $2,075 on March 1, 2026.

Watch out: premium processing speeds up the decision, not the outcome. A weak application is still weak.

6. Stay put and stay in status while you wait

Do not leave the U.S. while your application is pending. Travel abroad generally abandons the request.

Watch out: if you are in B-1/B-2 status, do not enroll or start classes until USCIS approves the change. Doing so is a status violation.

7. Respond quickly to any request for evidence

Answer by the deadline on the notice and send exactly what was asked for, organized and labeled.

8. Start your program once approved

Your F-1 status begins on the approval date. F-1 activities such as practical training can begin once you are approved and within 30 days of your program start date.

Watch out: if approval comes after your program start date, your DSO will defer you to a later term. Study in the States confirms that F-1 applicants whose status was valid at filing do not need extra filings to bridge the gap to the new start date.

Common F1 change of status problems and how to avoid them

These are the problems that cause most delays and denials. Open each one to see how to avoid it.

Filing too late

USCIS can only change your status if it is still valid when your application arrives. If your I-94 date has passed or you have worked without authorization, you are out of status, and USCIS generally cannot approve the change except in limited circumstances beyond your control. Check your I-94 date on the CBP website the day you decide to switch, and file with time to spare.

Missing your program start date

USCIS processing for I-539 requests can take months. If your application is still pending when your I-20 start date arrives, your DSO will defer you to a later term. That is not a denial, but it can push your studies back a semester at schools with only two or three start dates a year. A school with frequent start dates shortens that wait.

Starting classes or work too early

Enrolling while you are still in B-1/B-2 status is a status violation, even if your application is pending. The same goes for starting CPT or any other F-1 activity before approval. Wait for the approval notice, then confirm your start date with the DSO.

B-1/B-2 visitors and the question of intent

If you entered as a visitor, USCIS may ask whether you planned to study before you arrived. The State Department uses a 90-day guideline for conduct inconsistent with your visa, and many school advisors recommend waiting at least 90 days after entry before filing. USCIS is not bound by that guideline, but a filing soon after arrival invites closer questions. Talk to an immigration attorney about your timeline before you file.

Requests for evidence

Requests for evidence on F-1 change of status cases often ask for clearer proof of funds or proof that you maintained your current status. Send complete, current bank statements and sponsor letters the first time, and include a short statement explaining why the program fits your background.

Traveling while the application is pending

Leaving the U.S. while your I-539 is pending generally abandons it. If you must travel, the alternative is to withdraw the application, apply for an F-1 visa at a U.S. consulate abroad and re-enter as a student. Decide which route you are taking before you book anything.

Denials

If USCIS denies your application, you need to leave the U.S. when your current status ends, or sooner if it has already ended. A denial can also come up in future visa interviews. This is why getting the filing right the first time matters more than getting it in fast.

Where professional F1 student visa support reduces errors

 

You are allowed to file Form I-539 on your own, and many students do.

Most change of status problems, though, come from decisions made before the form is filed: when to file, which start date to target and whether to change status in the U.S. or travel for a visa instead. That is where support pays off.

Who can legally help with an F1 change of status

This matters more than most students realize. According to USCIS, only a licensed attorney or an accredited representative of a Department of Justice recognized organization can give you legal advice or represent you on an immigration case. A notario, notary public or immigration consultant cannot, no matter what they advertise.

In practice, reliable support for a change of status usually comes from a few places working together.

  • A licensed immigration attorney reviews your history, advises on timing and risk, and prepares or reviews the filing.
  • Your school's DSO issues the I-20, sets and defers your program start date, and explains the F-1 rules once you are approved.
  • An enrollment partner such as EduConnect helps you pick the right program and start date, gets your admission and I-20 moving quickly, and keeps the school side of your timeline on track.

Signs an agency is not reliable

Walk away or ask harder questions if you see any of these.

  • It guarantees approval or promises to speed up USCIS. Only USCIS controls processing, apart from paid premium processing.
  • The person giving you legal advice is not a licensed attorney or accredited representative, or will not say which they are.
  • It asks for your original documents or tells you to sign a blank or incomplete form.
  • It tells you to start classes or work before your approval arrives.
